“UGHHHHH, what is this knot?!”
Aran flung his rope across the camp table, landing in a heap that looked nothing like a knot. Beside him, Alex didn’t even look up, just staring into his tangled mess like it had wronged him. Two of the most energetic sixth graders in the troop sat frozen in front of me, stumped and about ready to give up.
My job was to help them learn the bowline knot, which, according to the Scout Handbook, should be easy to tie. After their tenth attempt, I started to doubt that. Other Scouts were waiting for me to sign off their requirements, and I had shown Alex and Aran the same steps over and over, yet the knot kept collapsing. My voice sharpened, and I clenched my rope tightly. Even after sewing on the SPL badge months earlier, patience was still something I struggled with. Why wasn’t it working?
Then I remembered feeling the same way the year before.
In my sophomore year, I first learned about volumes of revolution in calculus. The textbook asked me to rotate a graph around a line. What? I tried to memorize the formulas, but there were too many of them and too many problem types to know which one to use. So I put the formulas away and looked at what was actually happening instead. When you rotate a small part of a curve around a line, it creates something like a donut. To find the area of each donut, you subtract the hole from the outer circle, just simple geometry. Add all the donuts together, and you get the whole shape. That’s all an integral was doing: adding up donuts. I didn’t need more formulas. I needed to understand what was going on underneath them.
Looking back at the ropes in front of me, I knew what I needed to do.
“Okay, let’s restart,” I said. “Show me how you tie it, but slowly.” While both Scouts tied it differently, they shared the same problem: they tried to follow the handbook's instructions without understanding how the knot’s parts worked together. They didn’t need new instructions. They needed a way to picture the knot.
“You guys know what rabbits are, right?”
“Doesn’t everyone?” Aran responded, and Alex started to giggle.
I held up the end of my rope. “Imagine this is a rabbit, and the rabbit lives in this hole,” I said, pointing to the loop. “This part standing up is the tree. The rabbit comes out of its hole, hops around the tree, and goes back into its hole.” I pulled the rope taut, snapping the knot into place. The giggles began to fade as they tried it themselves. Aran caught on first, and within seconds, he was guiding Alex’s hands through the loop. Moments later, Alex shouted, “I did it!” My hands started to loosen and a smile came over my face. I don’t think I’ve ever been prouder.
Now, whether rabbits actually live in holes or run around trees, I may never know. What I do know is that I’m always looking for the “rabbits” that make complex systems click. The same curiosity that helped me teach a knot has pushed me to explore how different parts, whether gears, motors, or mathematical models, work together to create something functional. 
Whatever I end up building in the years ahead, I hope I'm still the person in the room asking where the rabbit is hiding, patient enough to help someone else find it too.
